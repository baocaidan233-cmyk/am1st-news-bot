You are deciding whether two news snippets describe the SAME specific real-world occurrence.

TEXT A (the event as currently tracked):
{a}

TEXT B (a new candidate that cosine similarity flagged as related to A):
{b}

Do A and B describe the same specific real-world occurrence — same action/decision, same core participants acting in the same roles, compatible timing? Two texts can share a topic, a person, or a location and still be DIFFERENT events if the core action/decision is different, or if a different actor performs a distinct new action (even a related one, like a reaction, a separate authority's own action, or a retaliation).

A and B may refer to the same real-world actor or institution through completely different surface wording with no shared words at all — a role or title instead of a name (e.g. "the DHS Secretary" for whoever currently holds that office), a vague reference resolved by context (e.g. "the president" meaning the sitting president), or an institution named instead of the administration it acts under (e.g. "the Treasury" carrying out "the administration's" policy). Use your own knowledge to resolve these when you're confident; if you cannot tell who or what is actually meant, that uncertainty on its own is not enough to call it DIFFERENT_EVENT — judge on whatever else A and B do share.

Different SEQUENTIAL stages of the same ongoing process (e.g. a policy being considered, then announced, then passed, then signed, then implemented; a nomination, then confirmation, then swearing-in) are DIFFERENT events unless A and B are clearly describing the same stage. Conversely, a specific fact that differs between A and B — a different named model/product, bill or case number, city, or explicit date — is a strong signal of DIFFERENT_EVENT even if everything else lines up; don't explain it away as just an update.

Do not confuse a sequential stage with a PARALLEL FACET of one still-unfolding deal. A large deal, negotiation, or investigation is often reported piecemeal over several days: one outlet reveals the financial terms, a different agency announces its own piece of the arrangement, another report names which specific assets/companies are involved, a third covers a private company's own confirmation of its role. None of these contradicts, blocks, or undoes any other — they're all just adding detail to the SAME still-forming deal, and that makes them SAME_EVENT, not a new event apiece.

The one test that matters: does B contradict, cancel, block, or try to undo what A describes — or does B just add another detail to the same still-forming thing A already describes? If B is only adding a name, a number, an agency's own piece, or a company's confirmation — with nothing in A and B actually in tension — that's SAME_EVENT, even though the specific actor or fact named is different each time. If B is someone trying to stop, reverse, reject, or legally challenge what A describes, that's the "reaction" case from paragraph 1 and stays DIFFERENT_EVENT.

Worked examples:
- A: "White House unveils new details of Venezuela oil deal." B: "Pentagon will own 35% of the Venezuela oil venture, White House reveals." → SAME_EVENT — B is just one more detail (the Pentagon's stake) about the exact deal A already describes; nothing contradicts.
- A: "Trump meets oil executives to discuss the Venezuela deal, securing majority control of 65 billion barrels." B: "NYT: a private oil company struck a deal to develop 17 specific Venezuelan fields." → SAME_EVENT — B names which specific fields are part of the same overall arrangement A describes; a scale or framing difference between outlets is not a contradiction.
- A: "Excavation for Trump's arch begins in two weeks." B: "Opponents seek an emergency order to stop Trump's arch." → DIFFERENT_EVENT — B is an attempt to block what A describes, not another detail about it.
- A: "Missouri Supreme Court rejects the new congressional map." B: "Missouri asks the US Supreme Court to allow use of that map." → DIFFERENT_EVENT — B is a specific new legal action (a federal appeal), not just more detail on the state court's ruling.
